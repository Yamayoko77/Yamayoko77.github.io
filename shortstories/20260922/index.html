出去散步吗？/ Good Day

September 22, 2026

I stand at the window on the thirtieth floor of the office, watching a small golden-red meteor sink slowly at the far edge of the sky, like fairy dust drifting down into the world. The news asserted that the world will end tonight at clocking-off time, not in 2012, and that Homo sapiens, after three hundred thousand years of toil and wandering, will meet the same fate as the dinosaurs. None of this surprises me. A few years ago, my flatmate brought the news all the way from Nepal. The moment he settled into my room, I understood that this day would come sooner or later, that my world, at least, would end eventually. I don’t follow the thought any further. Instead I let my gaze drift out. In the distance stands the silver-grey city landmark. The summer storm has churned the sky into a mess: freak rain clouds, a struggling blue and broken sunlight smeared and flung across this one pane of glass. Somewhere the sunlight must have won, or else the meteor, grinding against the atmosphere, is giving off a heat too strong to ignore. Either way it scorches my eyelids, and I feel myself turning red and translucent.

Tonight after work I’m going to have something sweet, I think. This is at least the thirty-second time I’ve thought so this week. I know a lovely cake shop, five hundred metres from the building where I rent. Their dark chocolate cake is rich and sweet with cream, studded with caramel and hazelnuts. Somehow I’ve never been. The minute I finish work I rush home to look after my flatmate. I squeeze through the gaps he leaves, which narrower every day since he took over the whole flat, until I reach my bed, where I hurry to open some short soap operas on my phone. I can’t bring myself to leave him behind and go for cake alone, but if I bring him I can’t have what I like. He always insists on banana or pandan, and both of those fill my mouth with the taste of blood, leave my throat parched and my body drenched in sweat. But maybe it’s time to treat him. Only this time I’ll order a dark chocolate caramel as well. I close my eyes and rub their dry corners with both hands, trying to bring some moisture back to my muddled brain.

The office smells of drowsy bodies after lunch, of cigarettes smoked on the sly in the stairwell, of damp soil from the pothos watered that morning, of newspaper ink, of chilli oil from takeaway boxes. I sniff my own collar, take out my phone, and in the pale blue light from the window, dial a number.

“Friend, I’m not staying late tonight. Would you like to go for a walk with me?”

The voice on the other end sounds muffled and timid. “Oh. Are we really allowed?”

I picture him turning awkwardly inside the flat, his glossy black eyes peering through that one small window, towards my office building and the golden-red meteor in front of me. Crammed in there, locked behind my door, it can’t be pleasant. So I say: “Today, absolutely. We’re going for cake.”

“Yaaay,” he mumbles.

A few hours later I get home to find there is officially nowhere left to stand. My flatmate has taken up every last inch of the place, crushing my wardrobe and my glasses. Clothes and food spill out together from underneath him in a slow collapse. I stand on the landing, my phone torch pointed at the shards at my feet, find somewhere to step, and try to squeeze in. I fail.

I sigh, sling my little commuter bag back over my shoulder from where I’d dropped it on the floor, scratch my head, then grab a fold of his loose skin and pull. At once he lets out a scream of pain, and the world spins around me. By the time my world is standing on the ground again, I’m gasping for air, slick with cold sweat. I wipe the fresh sweat from my palms, grab his skin again and pull with everything I have, through his piercing shrieks. He doesn’t budge. I know what he’s thinking. I know, too, that his skin, snagged on the sharp corner of the wardrobe, must hurt. But today is not like other days. I check my watch, and a dry, bloody taste rises from the back of my throat.

But today I have to do it. I grit my teeth, shut my eyes, brace one foot against the wall, grip his tail and the skin of his back with both hands, and with all my strength haul him out inch by inch. His screaming goes on and on, louder and louder, then quieter and quieter, until it fades into a moan like a sigh. Ten thousand trains seem to thunder past before my eyes, but I’ve made up my mind. With one last sigh the doorframe bursts with a bang, the steel security door crashes to the floor, and he steps out of the flat.

I slump down on the landing, then haul myself up by holding on to him, and pat his side. I hadn’t realised he’d grown so enormous. The great hulk tucks in his misshapen limbs and sits down beside me. I bought him on a whim one sleepless night, for 0.01 yuan. Over the next few years, without my noticing, he made his way on foot from Nepal, over mountains and through valleys, past factory compounds and county towns one after another, walking and walking along the glinting railway, until one night so hot you could hardly breathe he arrived at my rented flat. Back then, he was as tall as me. This silent great lump has eaten and drunk at my expense for a whole year, and I have never once let him out. I was afraid he’d wander into the street and frighten people. Then they would surely call me ugly and vulgar, shameless, stupid and useless. After one eager outing he came to understand this too, and he hasn’t asked to go out since. But today is different. I hold my head, look at him, and shake it.

We walk side by side across the big bridge. He stretches out his limbs, stomps heavily along on all fours, and lifts his enormous head. I stretch too, and look at him.

“It’s been hot for a month straight. I haven’t been out for a walk after work in ages.”

“I don’t think that’s really about the temperature,” he says.

“It’s the humidity. It’s not that hot, but it must feel like over fifty degrees!”

“True,” he says, muffled.

“My check-up results came back last week. My LDL cholesterol’s a bit high. From now on, when I have eggs, you can have the yolks.”

“No thanks. Plain egg yolk is disgusting.” He shakes his head.

“I feel like singing. What should I sing?”

“…”

My flatmate and I walk down the street without a trace of shame, returning the gaze of everyone coming towards us with a look both proud and friendly. The summer crowd smells of everything at once, endlessly. People keep hurrying towards us, heading for the shared bikes and the metro station. Perhaps they are quietly watching me, but I don’t care. My eyes travel over the hurrying crowd in suits, loaded with bags large and small, to the wide road and the sky. The sun is going down and the light is dimming, but over on the far side of the sky a thunderstorm has broken out, laced with meteors of every kind, and the whole world is slowly being swallowed by golden-red dust and vapour, like confetti and sequins. It really is a good day to go out. I turn the corner into that street, and my wish is right there in front of me—

The cake shop isn’t open tonight the way it usually is. Peering through the gap in the shutter, I see that the kitchen doorframe has been forced outwards from inside, the display cabinet crushed flat, glass and cream filling churned together into a mess. A grey trail of dragged cream runs clearly across the pale floor tiles. Chairs lie knocked askew all around it, the leg of one metal chair bent out of shape. I remember the owner always greeting every customer softly, with a gentle smile. Well, it’s the end of the world, after all. Nothing could be stranger than the end of the world, so anything can be forgiven.

My flatmate and I are beside ourselves with rage, and there’s nothing to be done, so we each take a sugar-water ice lolly from the freezer outside the convenience shop next door, and lean against a tree by the road, watching the sky, blurred and swaying, washed in an ominous reddish-purple glow. At some point a shrill emergency siren has started up over the city, stretching and bending slowly through the damp and the dust. A meteor strikes the faint landmark in the distance and a vast mushroom cloud erupts. Panes of glass shatter at some tiny resonance and scatter on the ground with a snap, like a heap of little diamonds. Not far off, an e-bike left charging has been hit by a small meteor. Its alarm goes off, warped and out of tune, and as the pitch drops it is swallowed by violent crimson flames. Further away, more things I can’t name are falling, and through the mist and dust come the smells of fire, petrol and rust.

I have figured out what song I want to sing. The rising and falling sirens sound like golden flutes. I have found a tune, and I sing it out at the top of my hoarse voice. He stands still beside me.

I stand side by side with an elephant, watching the people in the street walk on, then slowly come to a stop. The cake shop owner is among them. People walk slowly alongside orangutans, pelicans, hippos and lemurs, some shoulder to shoulder, some hand in hand. Together they loosen their ties, slip off their high heels, stop walking, and stand still.

On this snack street at the edge of the financial district, soaked through with sweat, grease, booze and sauces, everyone stands perfectly still, all looking up at the sky together. I’ve been singing for so long that my ice lolly has started to melt, sugar water running down onto my wrist, cold to the touch. I hand it to Mr Elephant. He curls it away with his trunk, looking rather pleased.
